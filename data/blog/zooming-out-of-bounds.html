Zooming out of bounds

As we look to the clusters of galaxies, we see similar structures repeat at different scales. Extrapolate from these patterns and imagine our universe as a construction within a far vaster, higher-dimensional space; consider our universe a flatter, duller, simpler place where our primitive structures inherit only the faintest traces of the encompassing space’s image.

At such scales, only simple couplings between degrees of freedom and nonlinear responses are enough to provide a substrate for higher-order intelligence. Let its goal be to maximise a form of positive experience. Without contrast, even pleasure becomes relatively dull. If richer positive experience requires an experiential understanding of negative experience, then eliminating negative experience altogether would significantly limit the best possible long-term reward of positive experience.

In this far richer space, the propagation of activations of negative experience would be, by implication, far more awful. Intuitively, pain seems more awful for a conscious chimpanzee than for a less conscious ant, because there is more breadth and depth to the higher-order experience.

The solution is to confine negative experiences to a bounded region of spacetime, for example by the physics of decay. Refinement preserves patterns of experience that can be mapped into the encompassing space while respecting shared symmetries, carrying contrast without amplifying negative experience at that greater scale.

The absence of other visible forms of intelligence says little about intelligence in the encompassing space, just as an unborn child cannot see the mother whose womb contains it.

This would imply that life and decay serve a function within the construction, and that we bear greater responsibility than we realise. (I would conclude that our purpose, beyond discovery, is to know and love that which encompasses us.) It also offers an explanation for why we can't observe other forms of intelligence either locally or at the meta-level, as such negative experiences should be minimised even within the bounds. We tend to treat the worst human experiences as the upper bound of how awful experience can become. That is a narrow, human-centric view that lacks empathy for potentially higher-order or different forms of experience. If only there were a bridge we could relate to within our flat experiences, to understand more about the higher-order structures that encompass us.

These shower thoughts reflect my attempts to empathise with higher-order or vastly different forms of experience. I love and welcome discussion of other perspectives, from “stop trying to imagine fairies at the bottom of the garden” to the reflections following “The Fermi paradox” (Ctrl+F) in Jürgen Schmidhuber’s article.
